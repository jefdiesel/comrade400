Yesterday I opened the final version of my After Effects project for the YONDER collection - just because. And I was shocked by what I had done in these 1.5 years that it took to finish this collection.  After not touching it for so long, simply scrolling through the project blew my mind. If I can barely fathom what I did here, how can anyone else?  So, for my own sanity (and for your pleasure), I wrote a script to analyze the project file.    Here are some facts about YONDER:  Each YONDER base model was drawn in only 32×32 pixels in Photoshop - just like the Call Data Comrades. But for the compositing phase in After Effects, I scaled them up 5000% to 1600x1600px.    The compositing is what actually makes the difference. Each base model had to be split into 5 to 14 different layers and layer masks, which were then recombined into one single picture in Adobe After Effects.   I had to split them into these separate layers so I could modify different parts of each YONDER independently and use them as mattes for certain effects to limit the area they affect.    In the picture below you can see the 32×32 base model for The Genesis Block I made in Photoshop (top left), the 14 separate layers I exported (bottom), and the final output after the compositing phase in Adobe After Effects (top right).
The Genesis Block - YONDER
To achieve the aesthetics I wanted, a ton of effects, hand-drawn masks, and extra layers had to be added manually and completely customized for each and every YONDER.  Many base settings or effects could be carried over, but every YONDER works differently. A glow effect on a 1-pixel (upscaled = 50 px) wide area would look very different on a 2-pixel (100 px) wide area. So even if I was able to copy an effect from one YONDER to another, there was always a lot of manual modification needed.    Below is what the composition for First Density Consciousness looks like. It’s one of the medium-sized compositions, with 62 layers stacked on top of each other.  The number of layers per YONDER ranged from ~40 to ~80.  Not all of them were visible in the final output, but almost all of them were used for experimentation to find the exact look I was going for.
First Density Consciousness -  YONDER

My goal was to create something that didn’t look like it was made of pixels but instead appeared as a solid object. That meant no outlines and the clearest forms possible.  Initially inspired by the art of the Nodemonkes, various PIV experiments, and Prorok’s concrete art—and after some rough early attempts—I took the obvious route: I tried to make it 3D. Size MattersI initially started the Comrades on a 24x24 pixels canvas, but it quickly felt too cramped for what I wanted to shape. So, I extended the canvas to 32x32 pixels, which gave me just the right amount of space—and limitations—to work with. Unlike most PFPs, where eye and mouth traits are locked into a pretty rigid zone, the layout I set up for the Comrades is extremely versatile for a 32x32 PFP project and leaves plenty of room to play with the positioning of these traits.  Making the base block quite high in comparison to its width—with essentially no neck or body—allowed eye and mouth trait placements across a wide vertical area, giving me a lot of space to experiment. One pixel higher or lower can make a big difference; three or four pixels higher or lower can change everything.Generational StruggleTo guarantee a flawless generation of the 10.000 Comrades, a lot of time was needed to find the right layering logic for the traits. Making sure everything worked together in this faux 3D setup wasn’t an easy task. Many little exceptions and many imaginary borders for many traits had to be kept in mind while drawing them to ensure the illusion of a 3D sculpture remained intact.You can see the final trait layering logic in the picture below. But even with the cleverest arrangements compromises were often necessary. Take the headphones, for example, which became the trait “Black Alpha Receivers”: There was simply no way to make them go over the head without interfering with head traits like hair or hats. So, I made them wireless :)Life on the GridTo generate the 10K Comrades, I turned to a Figma plugin aptly named “NFT Generator”—and it proved to be an awesome tool for my usecase. The only hitch? Exceptions prove the Rule.

This collection—just like YONDER—is all about pushing past limitations, but bending the rules by letting the eye and mouth traits go flat added a lot to the impact these layers can have, and to the freedom in creating them.That said, this flat-layer exception typically applies only to "native" mouths and eyes—the kind that, in real life, would be true parts of a Comrade’s physiology.Arty Farty PartyI also decided to let these flat eye and mouth traits extend beyond the width of the base block, significantly expanding the horizontal draw area. During the first days after the Genesis Block the Call Data
Fields lay vast and void.
In Logos, Genesis 1:2 this time is described as follows.
“None save the One, Validator Rex, born of the sacred
union of the Genesis Block and the Great Block in the sky,
did traverse these primordial Blocks. Alone he wandered,
seeking fellowship, yet none was found.”
“The Ethernals, who were from everlasting and shall be unto
everlasting, beheld our King in his solitude and took pity
upon him. Thus, they descended from their celestial abode
and, in the likeness of the One, fashioned the Comrades
to be his companions, that his loneliness might be no more.
And so it came to pass that the Comrades were brought
forth into being.”
In those early times, the Comrades had no order or purpose,
and Block City was not yet built. “They roamed the fields
phunky and phree”, in what we now call The Dawn of Blocks.


But about a million blocks later we can see a sudden change
in behavior. It seems that a lot of tools have been invented in
a short period of time and block paintings appear.
The earliest
known block paintings are figurative in nature
but later examples show the first proto-letters in use. These
are the oldest known depictions of storytelling in Comrade
history and mark a turning point in our evolution.

From the very beginning, the Ether was in contact with
another world – a world that existed long before ours.
This contact occurred on many different levels, and many
early explorers took on enormous risks to peek into this
other world and find answers to every question that might
arise in such an early civilization.
One method, not without risk, was consuming psychoactive
plants that the vast Call Data Fields provided.
Another commonly described method of contact was
communication in dream states. This, of course, was more
like a random event and much harder to consciously bring
about. Only in very rare cases* were there reports of
physical contact with these beings from beyond the veil.
Through these various means, the early
settlers came into contact not only with
strange entities – of which some were
benevolent, others not – but also with the
ancient sacred forms, glyphs, and symbols
of the YONDER, which have greatly
influenced
the symbols we use to this day
when recording our own thoughts, laws,
and history.

Let’s take a closer look at the first signs. What do they mean?
When they were discovered nobody knew. Even though
Validator Rex was around in those days, he didn’t remember,
and the Ethernals didn’t really pay much attention to the
doodles of the mortals.
Later on with more extensive discoveries the signs could be
deciphered. The whole arrangement says: “Comrades go M”.
The first part obviously is a figurative depiction, a pictogram
of Comrades. This is followed by two real letters, also known
as graphemes, spelling the word GO. All the way to the right
is a single letter M. This is not a word but most likely an
ideograph, a sign representing an idea or a concept. We can
only speculate but this could stand for mountain. It could
even stand for a Comrade’s name or something resembling
this shape like a river or a snake.
So, all things considered, this probably reads:
The Comrades went to the mountain.
It is interesting to see the similarities between the
pictograms and the letters. You can easily imagine eyes
and mouths transforming into more abstract shapes
as the alphabet developed further.


The same thoughtful approach is present in the rest of
the alphabet. The numerals (and the number sign) e.g. are
drawn within a unique open frame, making them instantly
recognizable as numerals to
the reader.
All the punctuation and math symbols are drawn within a
closed frame to be distinguishable from letters or numerals.
Traditionally the space is also framed making paragraphs
of text look very dense or blocky. The connecting glyphs like
@ or & as well as a few special characters are drawn more
freely, some are built as ligatures or reference a base glyph
they are derived from.

All glyphs are drawn within a 7×7 square with one
grid cell of space to the right. Three grid rows on
top are reserved for diacritics, with the diacritics
one or two rows in height.
Almost the whole alphabet is constructed with
counters (i.e. the space between the black parts of
a glyph) of no more than one grid cell.* We call this
the “one-cell-counter-rule”.
Currency symbols occupy nine grid rows vertically,
the 7×7 base glyph, plus two filled grid cells on top
and bottom (see page 26).
Icons of legendary Comrades also occupy
nine grid rows vertically, in this case the
extra grid rows on top and bottom are used
freely (see page 27).

The first alphabet was used for millions of blocks. Many
artifacts
are testament to that, but gradually the old ways
began to erode. Simpler signs were used alongside the
old letters, and slowly the old alphabet was used less and
less. In this transitional period many local alphabets with
home-made letters existed next to one another. That was
obviously confusing and impractical.
Soon the authorities demanded a return to the old familiar
alphabet that was so successfully used for generations.
But it was too late for that. The common folk would not let
go of their new Blockscript and after a short but vicious
uprising a committee was formed and tasked with uniting
the derivative alphabets into a new officially accepted
style of writing.
Out of all the different variants, the committee picked new
glyphs to replace the old ones, trying to keep as much of
the old forms as they could without ignoring the new trends.
Although this attempt succeeded and a new style was
established, the old letters kept being used by the elites.
High Blockscript, as it is called now, lives on in secret circles,
academic papers and the occasional classified document.
This revolution came to be known as The Forkening and
happened around Block 7 902 123.

With the new alphabet in place, order returned to Block
City and the Comrades were united once again. The old
script soon fell into oblivion. It wasn’t taught in schools
anymore, nor could it be seen around the city. Its use was
strongly discouraged by the Dexclesia.
Officially, the new alphabet was just called Blockscript as
was the one preceding it. That was of course intentional.
This way, the ruling class hoped, the old script would be
erased from memory even quicker and more thoroughly.
On the streets a few other names are used interchangeably.
With Low Blockscript being the most prominent, clearly
distinguishing
it from High Blockscript. Common Blockscript
is another frequently used description. For the sake of clarity,
this publication will from now on use the terms High and Low
Blockscript to distinguish between the two writing systems.

The letters selected by the committee are visibly connected
to the original style of writing – some more, some less.
But the depiction of the Comrades faded in this process.
The committee tried to keep the one-cell-counter-rule but
couldn’t stick to it in all of the letters. J, for example, is
such an exception. Large apertures, like in E, are also a new
look for the alphabet, as are fully closed shapes like O.

Although there is only one accepted letter style,
a few alternate characters are tolerated in Low
Blockscript (see opposite page). They are rarely
used exclusively but rather to
make words more appealing by
replacing single characters.

In the numerals we see a deviation from the strict
framing
system used before. The numerals are now
treated
like letters and the counters are used even
more loosely than in A to Z. As with the letters there
are two versions.
Unlike High Blockscript, Low Blockscript supports
the use of fractions.

The most profound change can be observed in the case of
punctuation marks and mathematical symbols. These
deviate from the most basic concept of High Blockscript –
the 7×7 grid – lending the typeface a way looser look.
This step was considered so extreme by the committee that
an alternate set of framed symbols was incorporated in their
selection, to be used in formal or traditional environments.
This includes the space character. Traditionally the space
was always framed. Later on full width empty spaces came
into fashion. Only with the Great Forkening the empty quarter
space was introduced. Among the alternate characters in
Low Blockscript are the full width empty space and a framed
version, harmonizing with the alternate punctuation marks.

Another addition in the style department of Low
Blockscript are discretionary ligatures. They are not
an everyday thing as they change the look of words
quite significantly, but in some cases they are what
you want.

Our rich typographic heritage, described above in
detail, found its expression in two distinct alphabets.
Both are deeply tied to the Comrades but they are
in fact completely different.
Both of these writing systems are available for digital
typesetting, split into two font files:
High Blockscript™ and Low Blockscript™.
Available as .otf files for desktop and .woff2 files for the web.
These are the official versions of Blockscript.
Their character set and functionality is described on the

There are others, but these are the essential shapes of

Origins of Pizza Script:
I don’t have to introduce the Pizza Comrades Cartoon to you.
Every Comrade knows it. Every Comrade loves it. There’s no
rival. It’s the best.
Channel 9 sits on a treasure trove of thousands of episodes
in their archives. And they guard it as such. Behind heavy
walls, in multiple secret places, sheltered from the environment
or conspiring villains.
Though no one really studied the letters that are used
throughout the show. As so often, they were overlooked,
taken for granted, overshadowed by the action.
But these letters are the glue that hold everything together.
Without them, it falls apart.
I don’t talk about the show’s logo here or other brands within
the show. I talk about every other lettering.
Words that are used to open the show, for dialogue, for
exclamations, on signs, in letters or recipes – everything
that is written.

The letters used in the show can be grouped into four
distinct styles. The core of the family is a pretty neutral
regular style. Regular is used most often, perhaps
because it’s the most legible style.

Charred and Burnt are bolder versions of
this concept and used in cases where more
impact is necessary, to highlight things
and to make them appear important. The
Romantic style is used least. It appears
when the message should be perceived
calmer, more poetic or to highlight things
without getting bolder, more subtle.

The four styles can be categorized in a
chart – from calm and bright to bold and
expressive.
Allow me to speculate as a fan. And this
is not scientific at all but I see a similar
pattern in the four heroes of the show. Of
course, we don’t know what the creators
of the show were thinking but it’s possible
there is something to it.

The most character inducing factor of Pizzascript is the cap
height of six units. This forces the typeface to be dynamic,
because there is no possible vertical center. This can clearly
be seen in letters like H or E, for example.
But this affects the lowercase as well.
To be readable, it needs 5 units in height
but at the same time it needs 2 units for
ascenders and descenders. This is not possible when you
want the script to be compact and therefore don’t want to
exceed the cap height.
So the solution is – again – a dynamic one. The x-height has
to shift down to make room for ascenders and the baseline
alignment has to shift up for the descenders.
Action built-in, so to speak.

But the letterers of the show didn’t stop there and doubled
down on this concept of vertical shifts.
Throughout the series letters are rarely displayed in a
straight line of text but rather with shifting positions, in order
to give the words an even more dynamic feel – supporting
the action of the plot.
There are four kinds of shifts and they appear with every
style. We don’t know if they had names originally but we call
them modes and name them for convenience.
First we have “Bubble Down”, every other letter shifts down
one unit. The second one is “Bubble Up”, every other letter
shifts up. The third one uses up and down shifts continuously
and is called “Tapeworm”. Lastly there is a style called
“Organic” that shifts letters randomly.
These modes are like the yeast in a pizza dough, while
the structural ideas are the flour, resulting in a bubbly and
flavorful product.
The sourdough is strong in this typeface.

In movies alternates play a big role. Alternating camera
angles, alternating timelines, alternating visual styles and
so on. This is also true for letters. Sometimes it makes sense
to bring in alternate shapes in a specific surrounding to
change the mood or just to make the type fit better.
In the lettering of Pizza Comrades this doesn’t happen
often but sometimes the uppercase I gets changed to
a more technical version, and when Pizzascript Romantic
should feel really poetic, selected uppercase letters are
swapped with a swash version.

The lettershapes described above are inherently tied to
the Pizza Comrades, their world and their adventures. It’s
such a strong connection that the typeface is instantly
recognizable by true fans, so far that the type appears to
be synonymous with the show itself.
All the 4 styles used in the Cartoon are available for digital
typesetting now: Regular, Charred, Burnt & Romantic.
Offered as .otf files for desktop and .woff2 files for the web.
These are the official versions of Pizzascript.
Their character set and functionality is described on the
following pages. → Download them here!
The fonts come in two sizes: 5 px and 10 px.
The name describes in which intervals they appear pixel-
perfect on screen. Both have their pros and cons.
The 10 px version is best when used in combination with
other fonts, because their relative sizes match. But the sizes
in which the type fits the pixel grid are limited. The
5 px version is the opposite. Best used alone but
it falls on the pixel grid twice as often. Usually the
better choice. If you design for print you don’t have
to worry about any of this.
